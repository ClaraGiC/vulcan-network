Research

Our research

How the cognitive abilities of vultures shape their behaviour.

Why cognition?

Vultures live in a world that is changing fast: birds released into unfamiliar landscapes, new wind farms and power lines, shifting food supplies. How well they cope depends partly on cognition: the way an animal takes in, stores and uses information to decide what to do. Understanding it helps explain why vultures behave as they do, and why some do better than others.

Cognition

What we mean by cognition

We look at four parts of how a vulture deals with information.

Perceive

What does the bird notice? Wind and thermals, a carcass on the ground, other vultures circling, a power line ahead.

Photo: eg6da6 / Pixabay

Learn

How does experience change its behaviour? A young bird discovers where food is, which routes work, and what is safe, on its own or by following others.

Photo: MatthiasWaldl / Pixabay

Remember

What does it keep, and for how long? Feeding sites, roosts, routes and places where something went wrong.

Photo: Jecqan / Pixabay

Decide

How does it choose between options? Stay or leave, follow the group or go alone, cross the sea or take the long way round.

Photo: u_8w6yutz7 / Pixabay

But in ever-changing environments, flexible behaviour requires more. When a feeding site closes, does a bird keep returning, or does it switch? When a wind turbine appears on a familiar route, can it override the habit and fly around it? When food arrives on a fixed schedule, does it turn up in advance? Researchers call the mechanisms behind this executive control.

All of this changes as a bird grows up. For young vultures, the way they are raised, in the wild or in captivity, may shape not only what they learn but also how they think.

Our approach builds partly on a framework for reading cognition from tracking and other sensor data, which we are developing as a review paper (see Projects).

Methods

How we study it

No single method can capture all of the above. That is why we combine three approaches.

Tracking data

GPS and other sensor data of four European vulture species, collected over 20 years by partners across Europe. The birds are wild-hatched, captive-bred or rehabilitated, and range from juveniles to adults.

Field experiments

We change things in the field, such as when food is provided, to test what wild vultures learn and anticipate.

Models

We compare simulated birds with different cognitive strategies to real tracks.

Projects

Current projects

Released and wild vultures around human infrastructure

Do released and wild-hatched vultures avoid or approach human infrastructure, and does this change as they gain experience? We start with bearded vultures and will extend to cinereous and Egyptian vultures, analysing each species separately.

Feeding site use

Do vultures anticipate when and where food is provided, and which cues do they use? This will involve a combination of tracking, modelling and field experiments.

A cognitive framework for avian movement

A review paper on how cognition can be read from tracking and other sensor data in wild birds: what has been shown so far, which measures are informative, and which questions remain open.
